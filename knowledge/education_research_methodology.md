1. EDUCATION RESEARCH: PURPOSE AND SCOPE

Education research uses systematic methods to investigate teaching, learning, educational leadership, school management, education policy, and educational outcomes.

A research study should establish a clear problem, justify its significance, formulate research questions or hypotheses, select an appropriate methodology, collect credible evidence, analyse the evidence, and draw conclusions that are supported by the findings.

The research design must follow the research question. A method should not be selected simply because it is familiar or convenient.

2. RESEARCH PROBLEMS, QUESTIONS AND OBJECTIVES

A research problem identifies an educational issue, evidence gap, inconsistency, or practical difficulty that warrants systematic investigation.

A research question states what the study seeks to find out. Research objectives specify the actions required to answer the research question.

Example research question:
What is the relationship between teacher digital competence and student achievement in public secondary schools in Kilifi County?

Possible general objective:
To examine the relationship between teacher digital competence and student achievement in public secondary schools in Kilifi County.

Possible specific objectives:
1. To determine the level of teacher digital competence in the selected schools.
2. To establish student achievement levels using a clearly defined measure.
3. To examine the association between teacher digital competence and student achievement.
4. To assess whether the observed association remains after accounting for selected confounding variables.

These are proposed examples, not evidence that the research has been conducted or that the relationship exists.

3. VARIABLES AND OPERATIONAL DEFINITIONS

An independent variable is a predictor or explanatory variable used to examine variation in an outcome. In non-experimental research, this does not necessarily mean that the variable causes the outcome.

A dependent variable is the outcome the study seeks to explain or predict.

A confounding variable is a factor associated with both the explanatory variable and the outcome that may distort their observed relationship if inadequately addressed.

A control variable is a variable included in an analysis to account for differences that could influence the estimated relationship. A control variable is not automatically a confounder; its role depends on the research question and causal assumptions.

An operational definition explains precisely how a concept will be measured.

Example:
- Independent variable: teacher digital competence, measured using a justified digital competence instrument.
- Dependent variable: student achievement, measured using clearly specified assessment scores or another defensible indicator.
- Possible confounders: student prior achievement, socioeconomic circumstances, school ICT infrastructure, teacher experience, and school-level differences.

Researchers must justify each variable and explain how it will be measured. Do not assume that an instrument is valid, reliable, or appropriate without checking its evidence and suitability.

4. QUANTITATIVE, QUALITATIVE AND MIXED-METHODS APPROACHES

Quantitative research uses numerical data to describe patterns, estimate relationships, compare groups, or evaluate hypotheses.

Qualitative research investigates experiences, meanings, perspectives, processes, and contextual explanations using data such as interviews, observations, and documents.

Mixed-methods research integrates quantitative and qualitative evidence to address complementary aspects of a research problem. The study should explain why both forms of evidence are necessary and how they will be integrated.

The research question, theoretical position, data requirements, available resources, ethical considerations, and intended contribution should guide the choice of approach.

5. RESEARCH DESIGNS

A descriptive design characterises the features, experiences, conditions, or distributions of a population or phenomenon.

A cross-sectional design collects data at a particular period. It can describe current conditions and investigate associations, but it generally provides limited evidence about temporal order.

A correlational design examines the direction and strength of relationships among variables without necessarily manipulating them.

An explanatory correlational study seeks to understand observed relationships and may account statistically for selected alternative explanations. Statistical adjustment alone does not establish causality.

A longitudinal design collects data at multiple time points and can help investigate change and temporal ordering. It does not automatically eliminate confounding.

An experimental design manipulates an intervention and uses a comparison condition. Random assignment, where feasible and ethical, can strengthen causal inference.

A quasi-experimental design evaluates an intervention without full random assignment. Its validity depends on the design, comparison strategy, assumptions, and handling of alternative explanations.

A case study investigates a bounded case in depth, using one or more sources of evidence.

A qualitative interview or ethnographic design may be appropriate when the main objective is to understand participants' experiences, interpretations, or social processes.

No design is universally best. Researchers should explain how the selected design answers the research question and acknowledge its limitations.

6. POPULATION, SAMPLING AND UNIT OF ANALYSIS

The target population is the group to which the researcher intends the findings to apply. The accessible population is the portion that can realistically be reached under the study's constraints.

A sampling frame identifies the eligible units from which the sample is selected. The sample is the subset actually included in the study.

Probability sampling uses a known selection procedure that permits design-based inference under appropriate assumptions. Non-probability sampling uses other selection procedures, such as purposive or convenience sampling, and requires caution when generalising findings.

Sample size should be justified using the research design, population, expected effect or precision, variability, statistical power where relevant, sampling structure, and practical constraints. Do not invent a sample size or claim that a sample is representative without justification.

The unit of analysis is the entity about which conclusions are drawn, such as a student, teacher, classroom, school, or county.

In education research, students may be nested within classrooms and schools, and teachers may work across classes. The sampling structure and level of measurement must align with the analysis. Multilevel methods may be appropriate when the data structure and research question warrant them.

7. MEASUREMENT, VALIDITY AND RELIABILITY

Construct validity concerns whether the evidence supports the intended interpretation of a measure.

Reliability concerns the consistency or precision of measurement under specified conditions.

Content validity concerns whether an instrument adequately represents the relevant content or domain.

Criterion-related evidence examines how a measure relates to an appropriate external criterion.

Researchers should explain the origin of instruments, adaptation procedures, scoring rules, pilot testing, and available evidence of validity and reliability. If an instrument is adapted, its suitability should be evaluated for the new population and context.

A questionnaire, digital competence scale, examination score, or school record should not be described as valid or reliable merely because it has been used in another study.

8. DATA COLLECTION AND ANALYSIS

Data collection procedures should be linked directly to the research questions, variables, operational definitions, and ethical requirements.

Descriptive statistics may include frequencies, percentages, means, medians, measures of dispersion, and appropriate visualisations.

Correlation coefficients can describe associations between variables when their assumptions and measurement requirements are met. The Pearson correlation is appropriate for certain linear relationships involving quantitative variables; Spearman correlation may be useful for ranked data or suitable monotonic relationships.

Regression analysis can estimate relationships while accounting for specified variables. Model choice depends on the outcome's measurement scale, sampling design, assumptions, and research question.

Qualitative analysis may involve systematic coding, categorisation, thematic development, and interpretation supported by the collected evidence.

Mixed-methods analysis should explain how the quantitative and qualitative findings are compared, connected, or integrated.

Researchers should check missing data, outliers, measurement quality, model assumptions, possible selection bias, and the implications of the sampling design. Statistical significance alone does not establish practical importance or causality.

9. CONFOUNDING, BIAS AND CAUSAL INFERENCE

Confounding occurs when an alternative factor contributes to an observed association between the main explanatory variable and the outcome.

Possible sources of bias include selection bias, measurement error, non-response, recall error, social desirability, and inappropriate analytical decisions.

A plausible confounder should be selected using subject knowledge, the study's conceptual framework, and a defensible account of how variables relate. Adding every available variable to a regression model is not necessarily appropriate.

Researchers should distinguish association, prediction, and causation. Cross-sectional correlations cannot by themselves establish that one variable causes another. Strong causal claims require an appropriate design, defensible assumptions, and evidence addressing alternative explanations.

10. EDUCATION RESEARCH ETHICS AND DATA PROTECTION

Research involving people should protect dignity, privacy, autonomy, and welfare. Researchers should follow applicable institutional review requirements, research permits, consent procedures, safeguarding requirements, and data-protection rules.

Research involving minors requires particular care, including appropriate permissions, age-appropriate assent where applicable, and safeguards against coercion or harm.

Researchers should collect only data necessary for the stated purpose, protect identifiable records, restrict access appropriately, and report findings responsibly.

For research conducted in Kenya, the researcher should verify current requirements with the relevant institutional ethics review body and authorised research and education authorities. Requirements should not be assumed from an unverified summary.

11. LITERATURE REVIEW, CITATIONS AND RESEARCH INTEGRITY

A literature review should critically synthesise relevant research rather than merely list summaries. It should identify established findings, disagreements, limitations, methodological gaps, and the contribution proposed by the current study.

Use appropriate academic databases and primary sources where possible. Check that each cited source exists and that its authors, title, publication details, date, and findings accurately support the claim.

Do not invent references, quotations, DOIs, statistics, research findings, policies, or page numbers. If a source cannot be verified, explicitly identify it as unverified rather than presenting it as an established citation.

Distinguish published evidence, information supplied by the researcher, analytical inference, and proposed methodological recommendations.

AI-assisted research must preserve source traceability, confidentiality, human verification, and applicable disclosure requirements. AI-generated text is not itself evidence that a claim is true.

12. REPORTING FINDINGS AND ACKNOWLEDGING LIMITATIONS

A research report should describe the research problem, literature, framework, methods, results, discussion, conclusions, limitations, and implications as appropriate to the design.

Interpret findings in relation to the research questions and evidence. Report contradictory or null findings honestly.

Limitations may include restricted generalisability, measurement error, missing data, non-response, limited statistical power, selection bias, unmeasured confounding, and design constraints.

Recommendations should follow from the findings and their limitations. Proposed implications should be labelled as proposals where they have not been empirically evaluated.

13. GUIDANCE FOR AI-ASSISTED EDUCATION RESEARCH

When answering an education-research question, the assistant should:

1. Identify the research problem, question, objectives, and relevant concepts.
2. Distinguish information explicitly provided by the user from assumptions and proposed extensions.
3. Identify variables and provide operational definitions where appropriate.
4. Recommend a research approach and design that match the question, with a reasoned justification.
5. Explain sampling, measurement, data collection, and analysis options relevant to the design.
6. Identify plausible confounders, ethical issues, and methodological limitations.
7. Distinguish association from causation and avoid unsupported causal claims.
8. Never invent study data, citations, policy requirements, instruments, or research experience.
9. State when evidence or official requirements require verification.
10. Ask a clarifying question when missing information materially changes the methodological recommendation.
