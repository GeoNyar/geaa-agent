4.1 WHAT IS THERMODYNAMICS?
Thermodynamics is the study of energy changes and energy transfers associated with physical and chemical processes.
In chemistry, thermodynamics helps us understand:
•
where energy goes during a reaction;
•
whether a reaction releases or absorbs heat;
•
how much energy is transferred;
•
how chemical energy is related to other forms of energy.
Examples
When methane burns: 𝐶𝐻4+2𝑂2→𝐶𝑂2+2𝐻2𝑂
chemical energy is released mainly as heat.
When ammonium nitrate dissolves in water, the surroundings may become colder because energy is absorbed.
2
4.2 ENERGY IN CHEMICAL REACTIONS
Chemical substances contain chemical potential energy in their bonds and molecular structures.
During a chemical reaction:
•
some bonds in reactants are broken;
•
new bonds are formed in products.
Important principle
Breaking bonds requires energy.
Forming bonds releases energy.
Therefore, the overall energy change depends on the balance between:
energy required to break bonds
and
energy released when bonds form
4.3 SYSTEM AND SURROUNDINGS
In thermodynamics, it is useful to distinguish between the system and the surroundings.
System
Surroundings
The part of the universe being studied.
For example, in a reaction taking place in a beaker, the reacting chemicals may be considered the system.
Everything outside the system.
This may include:
•
the water;
•
beaker;
•
thermometer;
•
laboratory air.
4.4 EXOTHERMIC REACTIONS
An exothermic reaction is a reaction that releases heat energy to the surroundings.
As heat leaves the system, the surroundings usually become warmer.
Examples
•
combustion;
•
acid–base neutralization;
•
many oxidation reactions;
•
respiration.
For an exothermic reaction:
Δ𝐻<0
Example
𝐶𝐻4+2𝑂2→𝐶𝑂2+2𝐻2𝑂
This reaction releases energy.
4.5 ENDOTHERMIC REACTIONS
An endothermic reaction is a reaction that absorbs heat energy from the surroundings.
3
The surroundings usually become cooler.
For an endothermic reaction:
Δ𝐻>0
Examples
•
thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate;
•
some dissolving processes;
•
photosynthesis.
For photosynthesis:
6𝐶𝑂2+6𝐻2𝑂→𝐶6𝐻12𝑂6+6𝑂2
Energy from sunlight is required.

4.7 ENTHALPY CHANGE
Enthalpy change, represented by Δ𝐻, is the heat energy change associated with a chemical reaction under specified conditions, commonly at constant pressure.
Δ𝐻=𝐻products−𝐻reactants
Therefore:
Exothermic
𝐻products<𝐻reactants
so:
Δ𝐻<0
Endothermic
𝐻products>𝐻reactants
so:
Δ𝐻>0

4.8 ENERGY PROFILE DIAGRAMS
An energy profile diagram shows how energy changes as a reaction proceeds.
Exothermic reaction
The products have lower energy than the reactants.
Therefore:
Δ𝐻<0
Endothermic reaction
The products have higher energy than the reactants.
Therefore:
Δ𝐻>0
4.9 ACTIVATION ENERGY AND THERMODYNAMICS
Remember from Chemical Kinetics that activation energy is the minimum energy needed for a successful reaction.
An energy profile can therefore show both:
•
activation energy, 𝐸𝑎;
•
enthalpy change, Δ𝐻.
5
For example:
Important distinction
𝐸𝑎≠Δ𝐻
Activation energy determines the energy barrier for reaction.
Enthalpy change describes the overall energy difference between reactants and products.
4.10 EFFECT OF A CATALYST ON AN ENERGY PROFILE
A catalyst lowers the activation energy.
It does not change the overall enthalpy change.
Therefore:
Δ𝐻catalysed=Δ𝐻uncatalysed
but: 𝐸𝑎,catalysed<𝐸𝑎,uncatalysed
This connects thermodynamics with the chemical kinetics studied in Topic 3.
4.11 MEASURING HEAT ENERGY
Heat energy can be estimated using:
𝑞=𝑚𝑐Δ𝑇
where:
•
𝑞= heat energy transferred in joules (J);
•
𝑚= mass of the substance being heated (g);
•
𝑐= specific heat capacity (J\,g^{-1}\,^\circ C^{-1});
•
Δ𝑇= temperature change (∘𝐶).
Δ𝑇=𝑇final−𝑇initial

4.12 WORKED EXAMPLE 1
A student mixes two solutions. The temperature of 50.0 cm³ of solution increases from 22∘𝐶to 29∘𝐶.
Assume:
•

•

Step 2: Find temperature change Δ𝑇=29−22 Δ𝑇=7∘𝐶
Step 3: Apply the formula 𝑞=𝑚𝑐Δ𝑇 𝑞=(50.0)(4.18)(7) 𝑞=1463𝐽
Therefore: 𝑞=1.46×103𝐽

𝑞=1.46𝑘𝐽

In the example above, the temperature increased.

Therefore, the reaction released heat.
The reaction is: exothermic

4.14 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: COOLING
A reaction causes the temperature of 100 g of solution to fall from 30∘𝐶to 24∘𝐶.
Assume: 𝑐=4.18𝐽𝑔−1∘𝐶−1
Temperature change Δ𝑇=24−30=−6∘𝐶
Therefore: 𝑞=𝑚𝑐Δ𝑇 𝑞=(100)(4.18)(−6) 𝑞=−2508𝐽
The solution loses thermal energy.
The reaction has absorbed energy from the surroundings.
Therefore the process is: endothermic
When reporting the magnitude of heat absorbed, we may state:
2.51𝑘𝐽 absorbed
4.15 SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITY
Specific heat capacity is the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1∘𝐶.
The SI unit commonly used in these calculations is: 𝐽𝑔−1∘𝐶−1
Water has a relatively high specific heat capacity: 4.18𝐽𝑔−1∘𝐶−1
This is why water can absorb considerable heat without a very large temperature increase.
4.16 HEAT CAPACITY VS SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITY
Do not confuse these terms.
Heat capacity
The heat required to raise the temperature of an entire object or system by 1∘𝐶.
8
Specific heat capacity
The heat required to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1∘𝐶.

4.18 HESS'S LAW
Hess's Law states that the total enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided the initial and final states are the same.
In simple terms:
Overall energy change = sum of energy changes of individual steps
This is useful when the enthalpy change of a reaction cannot easily be measured directly.
Example
Suppose:
𝐴→𝐵
has:
Δ𝐻1=−40𝑘𝐽𝑚𝑜𝑙−1
and:
𝐵→𝐶
has:
Δ𝐻2=−60𝑘𝐽𝑚𝑜𝑙−1
Then:
𝐴→𝐶
has: Δ𝐻=−40+(−60) Δ𝐻=−100𝑘𝐽𝑚𝑜𝑙−1

4.19 BOND ENERGY
Chemical reactions involve breaking old bonds and forming new bonds.
Using average bond energies: Δ𝐻=energy required to break bonds−energy released forming bonds
Therefore:
Δ𝐻=Σ𝐸(bonds broken)−Σ𝐸(bonds formed)
Worked Example
Suppose:
•
bonds broken require 500𝑘𝐽;
•
bonds formed release 650𝑘𝐽.
Then: Δ𝐻=500−650 Δ𝐻=−150𝑘𝐽
The reaction is exothermic.
4.20 WHY BOND FORMATION RELEASES ENERGY
Atoms in a stable chemical bond are in a lower-energy arrangement than when separated.
When a bond forms, energy is released.
When the bond is broken, energy must be supplied.
Therefore: Bond breaking = energy absorbed Bond formation = energy released
4.21 THERMODYNAMICS AND BIOCHEMISTRY
Thermodynamics is important in biological systems.
Respiration: During aerobic respiration, glucose is oxidised and energy is released.
𝐶6𝐻12𝑂6+6𝑂2→6𝐶𝑂2+6𝐻2𝑂+energy
The released energy can be captured in molecules such as ATP.
Photosynthesis: Photosynthesis requires an input of energy, mainly from sunlight. 6𝐶𝑂2+6𝐻2𝑂→𝐶6𝐻12𝑂6+6𝑂2 Thus, thermodynamics helps explain the energy relationship between photosynthesis and respiration.

4.22 PRACTICAL APPLICATION
In an experimental investigation of heat change, careful measurement is important.
A typical procedure may involve:
1.
measuring a known volume of solution;
2.
measuring the initial temperature;
3.
mixing the reactants;
4.
recording the highest or lowest temperature reached;
5.
calculating Δ𝑇;
6.
calculating 𝑞;
7.
determining whether the process is exothermic or endothermic.
Important experimental considerations
•
Minimize heat loss to the surroundings.
•
Use appropriate insulation where possible.
•
Stir consistently.
•
Read the thermometer correctly.
•
Record temperatures accurately.
•
Use the correct units.
4.23 COMMON ERRORS
Error 1: "Exothermic means the reaction absorbs heat."
Incorrect!
Exothermic means heat is released by the system.
Error 2: "Endothermic reactions always feel cold."
Not necessarily.
The important criterion is that the system absorbs energy.
Error 3: "A catalyst changes ΔH."
Incorrect!
A catalyst changes the activation energy pathway but does not change the overall enthalpy change.
Error 4: "Bond breaking releases energy."
Incorrect! Bond breaking requires energy
Error 5: Forgetting the sign of Δ𝐻. Exothermic: Δ𝐻<0 Endothermic: Δ𝐻>0
11
Error 6: Using volume directly as mass without considering density.
If density is 1.0𝑔 𝑐𝑚−3, then numerically:
50𝑐𝑚3≈50𝑔
but this assumption should be stated.
